Lindner and Costa have a contract agreement. Now the certificates need to reach Lindner's supplier management system. This step is called a **transfer**. It has two parts, and a different building block does each part.

:::note[In this lecture]
- The two parts of a transfer: permission and delivery.
- Which building block does which part.
- How a consumer keeps receiving current data without negotiating again.
:::

## Two parts, two building blocks

Every participant has the same building blocks: a connector, a data plane, and its own business systems. A transfer divides the work between them in a fixed way.

| Part | Done by | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Permission** | The connectors | Lindner asks for the transfer. Costa checks the agreement and allows it. |
| **2. Delivery** | The data planes | The certificates go from Costa to Lindner. |

## Part 1: permission

Lindner's connector sends a **transfer request** to Costa's connector. The request names the contract agreement. Costa's connector confirms that the agreement exists and is still valid, and allows the transfer.

Neither connector touches a certificate. Their job is to authorize and to coordinate. Once the transfer is allowed, each connector tells its own data plane what to do.

## Part 2: delivery

Costa's data plane is the only component that reaches Costa's document store. It delivers the certificates to Lindner's data plane, which hands them to the supplier management system.

The delivery is protected by an **access token**: a short-lived key that Costa's side issues for this one transfer under this one agreement. Without a valid token, Costa's data plane hands out nothing.

The data goes straight from Costa to Lindner. It does not pass through the association, and the connectors do not see it.

## Staying up to date

Two years later Costa's ISO 9001 certificate is renewed. Costa replaces the PDF in its document store. It does not notify thirty customers.

Lindner's agreement with Costa is still in place. Lindner can ask for the certificates again under the same agreement, and it receives the renewed version. Lindner might do this on a schedule, or whenever a buyer opens Costa's supplier record.

This is the main practical difference from sending files. The agreement gives Lindner continuing access to the current data at its source. A copy in a mailbox can never do that.

Some use cases also need the provider to tell consumers that something changed. A provider can send a notification to the consumers that hold an agreement, and they then fetch the new data. The notification is a signal. The data still travels through the data plane under the agreement.

## What each side kept

Costa kept control. The certificates never left its systems except in response to an authorized request under a contract. Costa can see which agreements exist and which transfers took place.

Lindner gained reliability. It obtained the certificates from the supplier itself, with the supplier's identity verified, and can obtain the current version again at any time.

## Key takeaways

- A transfer has two parts. The connectors give permission, and the data planes deliver.
- The delivery needs an access token that is valid for one transfer under one agreement.
- Data flows directly from provider to consumer.
- An agreement can stay in place, so the consumer can return for current data without a new negotiation.
